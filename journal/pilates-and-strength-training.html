Pilates and Strength Training: Why the Best Routine Uses Both

June 19, 2026 4 min read By The Method Room

In short: Pilates builds control, stability, mobility and deep core strength. Heavier strength training adds the load that builds muscle and loads bones. Together they cover each other's gaps. A balanced week might include two reformer classes, one or two weights sessions and regular walking.

For a while, fitness felt like a choice between two camps: the calm, controlled world of Pilates and the heavier, louder world of the weights room. In 2026, more women are refusing to choose. ACSM's annual fitness trends survey puts balance, flow and core strength and functional fitness training in its top ten, and many people are now blending strength and mobility in the same week. The good news is that the two approaches complement each other beautifully.

What Pilates does well

  • Control and technique: slowing down teaches you to move precisely, which carries over to every other kind of exercise.
  • Deep core and stability: the small stabilising muscles around your spine, pelvis and shoulders get a lot of attention.
  • Mobility with strength: exercises load muscles through a full range of movement, not just a short one.
  • Body awareness and breath: knowing what you are doing and where you are in space helps you lift, run and move more efficiently.

What heavier strength training adds

Lifting weights (or using heavier resistance) provides a larger external load. Progressively challenging your muscles is how you build and maintain strength, and loading your skeleton is important for bone health. UK physical activity guidelines recommend muscle-strengthening activity on at least two days a week alongside aerobic exercise, and a regular Pilates habit can count towards that, especially in a strength-focused class. Heavier work simply extends the range of how much load you can handle.

Where the reformer sits in the middle

Reformer Pilates is a particularly good bridge between the two. The springs give you genuine, adjustable resistance, so you can build strength without losing the focus on control and alignment. A class like our Reformer Strength uses progressive resistance and tempo to build muscle endurance and full-body strength, while keeping form front and centre.

Why this matters even more right now

More people are using weight-management medications such as GLP-1s, a trend ACSM highlights in its 2026 report. When weight is lost, some of it can be muscle, which is one reason resistance training is widely recommended alongside any weight-loss approach. If this applies to you, speak to your prescriber or GP about exercise, and tell your instructor so they can tailor the class.

A simple week that blends both

  • Two reformer classes: one Flow for technique and rhythm, one Strength for load and endurance.
  • One or two weights sessions: focus on compound moves such as squats, hinges, presses and rows.
  • Daily movement: walking, taking the stairs, and a few minutes of mobility on rest days.
  • One real rest day: recovery is where your body adapts.

How to progress without overdoing it

Add challenge gradually: one extra spring, one slower tempo or one additional class a week, not all three at once. If your form slips, you feel unusually tired or your lower back is doing the work, scale back for a few days. Progress that you can sustain beats a burst that ends in a setback.

Frequently asked questions

Is Pilates enough for strength?

Pilates builds real strength, particularly in your core, hips and shoulders, and a Strength-focused reformer class adds progressive resistance. For maximal strength and bone loading, pairing Pilates with heavier resistance training works well.

Should I do Pilates or weights first on the same day?

If you do both on one day, many people prefer doing the heavier lifting first while fresh, with Pilates afterwards, or splitting them into separate sessions. Listen to how your body feels.

How many strength sessions do I need each week?

UK guidelines recommend muscle-strengthening activity on at least two days a week. Two reformer classes, plus one or two weights sessions, is a balanced starting point.

Will Pilates make me bulky?

No. Pilates builds lean, functional strength and improves posture and control. Gaining significant muscle size requires specific heavy training and nutrition.
