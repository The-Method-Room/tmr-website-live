Reformer vs Mat Pilates: Which Is Right for You?

May 22, 2026 4 min read By The Method Room

In short: Mat Pilates uses your bodyweight on the floor. Reformer Pilates uses springs and a sliding carriage to add adjustable resistance and support. Neither is better in every case: mat builds control and discipline, while the reformer makes it easier to scale strength work and be guided through good form. Many people do both.

If you have scrolled social media or tried to book a class lately, you will have noticed it: Pilates is everywhere. ClassPass named Pilates its most-booked workout of 2025, with reservations up 66% on the year before, and ACSM's 2026 fitness trends list places balance, flow and core strength in the top five. With so many people curious about it, one question comes up again and again: should you start with reformer or mat?

What is mat Pilates?

Mat Pilates is practised on the floor, using your own bodyweight for resistance. Classes often include small props such as a resistance band or a ball, but the mat itself is the main piece of equipment. The focus is on controlled movement, breath, alignment and deep core strength. Because there is nothing to assist you, it asks a lot of your stability and body awareness, which is why even simple-looking exercises can feel surprisingly hard.

What is reformer Pilates?

The reformer is a low, bed-like frame with a sliding carriage, adjustable springs, a footbar and straps. The springs provide resistance that can be made lighter or heavier, so the same exercise can be gentler or more challenging depending on what you need. The carriage supports your body, guides the movement and gives you instant feedback when your alignment drifts.

The key differences

  • Resistance: mat relies on bodyweight and gravity. Reformer uses springs, so you can add or remove load in small steps.
  • Support: the reformer can assist you (making an exercise more accessible) or challenge you (making it harder), while the mat leaves you fully unsupported.
  • Feedback: the moving carriage tells you straight away if you are rushing or compensating, which many beginners find helpful.
  • Range of exercises: the reformer allows you to work in lying, kneeling, standing and seated positions, with the legs and arms loaded in different ways.
  • Access: mat Pilates can be done almost anywhere. Reformer classes need a studio and are usually priced higher per session.

Which gives better results?

It depends on your goal, and honestly on consistency more than format. Mat Pilates is excellent for building control, core endurance and body awareness. Reformer Pilates makes it easier to apply progressive resistance, which is helpful if your aim is strength, shape and posture. If you want to feel your muscles working in a clear, guided way and be able to increase the challenge gradually, the reformer tends to make that simpler to do safely.

Which is better for beginners?

Many first-timers find the reformer more approachable than they expected. The springs can support you while you learn the movements, and a good instructor will set the resistance to suit you. Mat classes can be brilliant too, but without any equipment to guide you, small technique errors are easier to miss in a larger class. Whichever you choose, look for a beginner-specific class, small group sizes and an instructor who adjusts the work to your body.

Can you do both?

Absolutely, and lots of people do. Mat work teaches you to control your own bodyweight; reformer work lets you build on that with load and variety. If you have a regular reformer practice, a short mat sequence at home on non-studio days is a simple way to keep your core and posture work ticking over.

Frequently asked questions

Is reformer Pilates harder than mat Pilates?

Not necessarily. The reformer can make exercises easier or harder depending on the spring settings, so difficulty depends on how the class is set up. Many beginners find the guidance and support of the reformer easier to learn on.

Do I need to do mat Pilates before trying reformer?

No. You can start on the reformer straight away, especially in a beginner-focused class such as Reformer Foundations, where the equipment, setup and technique are taught from scratch.

How often should I do reformer Pilates?

Most people see steady progress with two to three classes a week. Starting with one or two and building up gradually is a perfectly good approach.

Is reformer Pilates suitable if I have an injury?

Often, yes, because the exercises can be adapted. Always tell your instructor about any injuries or conditions before class, and speak to your GP or physiotherapist first if you have any concerns.
