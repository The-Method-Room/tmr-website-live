Reformer Pilates for Perimenopause and Menopause: Strength, Balance and Confidence

July 17, 2026 4 min read By The Method Room

In short: Muscle and bone health matter more as oestrogen levels change. Reformer Pilates is low-impact, adjustable and good for strength, balance and body awareness, but it works best alongside walking, heavier resistance work and weight-bearing activity. Speak to your GP about symptoms and before starting if you have any concerns.

Perimenopause and menopause have finally moved out of the shadows. Women are talking about them openly, employers are paying attention, and wellness has begun to take them seriously. Exercise is one of the most useful tools you have during this time, and reformer Pilates can be a supportive part of it. Here is how, and where it needs a little help from other kinds of movement.

What can change in your body

As oestrogen levels fall, many women notice changes: sleep can be disrupted, joints may feel stiffer, energy and mood can fluctuate, and muscle mass and bone density tend to decline more quickly. Every woman's experience is different, and there is no single "right" way to move through it. What is widely agreed is that staying active and keeping your muscles and bones working is one of the best things you can do for your long-term health.

Why reformer Pilates can help

  • It is low-impact: the reformer is supportive and gentle on stiff or achy joints, while still being challenging.
  • The resistance is adjustable: springs let you build strength gradually and change the load on days when your body feels different.
  • It works on balance and stability: controlled single-leg and core work helps you feel steadier and more confident in daily life.
  • It builds body awareness and breath control: mindful movement gives you a reliable way to tune in, particularly when energy or mood is changing.
  • It includes pelvic and core awareness: an instructor can guide you in connecting breath with deep core and pelvic floor engagement.

What it cannot do on its own

NHS guidance on exercise in menopause highlights regular activity, including strengthening and weight-bearing exercise for bone health, and lists Pilates among good ways to stay active. Because bones respond to impact and heavier loading, Pilates works best as part of a wider mix: walking, some heavier resistance work, and weight-bearing activity where it is suitable for you. If you have osteoporosis or a family history, speak to your GP or physiotherapist about which exercises are right for you.

Making it work for you

  • Aim for consistency, not intensity: two to three classes a week builds more than one punishing session.
  • Include a Strength class: progressive resistance is the cornerstone of keeping and building muscle.
  • Choose your level honestly: Foundations and Flow are excellent places to build confidence before stepping up.
  • Tell your instructor how you feel: energy varies, and a good instructor will adjust springs and options on the day.
  • Add walking and recovery: a daily walk and a proper rest day support sleep and energy.

A supportive space matters

For many women, feeling comfortable is what keeps them showing up. A calm, women-only studio with small classes means you are not worried about being watched or keeping up, and you can ask questions freely. At The Method Room, classes are capped at eight reformers so your instructor can see you, adjust your setup and keep you challenged in the right way.

This article is for general information and is not medical advice. If you are experiencing symptoms that worry you, speak to your GP.

Frequently asked questions

Is Pilates good for menopause?

Many women find it helpful because it is low-impact, builds strength and balance, and supports body awareness. It works best alongside other activity such as walking and heavier resistance work.

Can reformer Pilates help with bone density?

Pilates can support strength and balance, but bones respond best to weight-bearing and heavier resistance exercise. Combine it with other activity and speak to your GP if you are concerned about bone health.

How often should I do Pilates during perimenopause?

Two to three classes a week is a good target for most people, and you can start with fewer. Rest and recovery matter just as much as the classes.

I'm new to exercise. Is it too late to start?

It is never too late. Our Foundations class teaches everything from setup to breathing, at your pace and with your instructor's guidance.
